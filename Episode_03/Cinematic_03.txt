A neon sign hangs above a narrow street like a dying star, its letters failing in sequence—GLITCH PALACE—buzzing, stuttering, then catching again with a tired electrical sigh. The light it throws isn’t clean; it’s grainy, uneven, and it paints the dust in the air into slow-moving glitter. Every flicker reveals a different version of the same ruin: cracked masonry, peeled posters fused to brick by years of rain, and a web of dead cables drooping from poles like vines that never learned how to grow.

The road is a patchwork of old asphalt and newer repairs—tar seams like black scars, potholes filled with gravel and broken glass. Rusted car husks sit half-swallowed by weeds at the curb, their windows long gone, their dashboards gutted, their frames turned into shelters for windblown trash. A traffic light hangs sideways over the intersection, dark and useless, its lenses clouded with grime. Beneath it, a crosswalk’s white stripes have faded into ghost marks, barely visible under soot and sand.

Power exists here the way fireflies exist—brief, localized, and never to be trusted. A few storefronts glow with rationed electricity: thin neon tubes, a wavering marquee, the faint pulse of a transformer somewhere behind a wall. The rest of the block is swallowed by shadow, lit only by spill and reflection—neon smeared across puddles, broken glass catching color like scattered gemstones, metal surfaces flashing cyan and magenta before falling back into black.

Nature has been patient. It has threaded itself into every seam the old world left open. Vines climb chain-link fences and disappear into second-story cracks. Moss spreads in the mortar lines. A sapling has forced its way up through a sidewalk slab, lifting concrete like a loose tooth. In the distance, the skyline is a jagged silhouette of skeletal towers—glass long since shattered, steel ribs exposed—each building a hollowed monument to a century that believed it could outpace time.

The air carries the smell of wet concrete, rust, and ozone—an electrical tang that clings after a flicker of current. Somewhere deeper in the block, bass vibrates through brick and rebar, not heard so much as felt, a low pressure that makes loose window frames tremble. The sound leaks out through gaps and vents, mixing with the soft rattle of a loose sign chain and the whisper of wind moving through empty floors.

Inside the structure, the light changes again. The glow becomes older—greenish, phosphor-tinted, the kind that belongs to tired screens and aging tubes. A corridor of cracked tile reflects it in broken fragments. Dust lies in drifts along the baseboards, disturbed in thin trails that suggest recent movement without revealing its source. The walls are layered with decades: paint over paint, graffiti over paint, old safety placards half-torn, their warnings meaningless in a world that no longer runs on compliance.

A side chamber sits off the main noise like a pocket of held breath. The hum here is different—less music, more machine. A tarp of stitched denim and bandanas hangs over a bulky shape, its edges frayed, its patches faded by smoke and time. The fabric lifts and settles with the building’s subtle currents, as if the room itself is exhaling.

Beneath the tarp’s edge, a thin line of sickly light leaks out—steady for a moment, then wavering, like a monitor trying to remember how to wake. The glow catches on exposed screws, on a tangle of hand-soldered wires, on the yellowed plastic of old keys. Dust motes drift through the beam and turn into slow, floating sparks.

And under everything—the neon buzz, the distant bass, the building’s hum—there’s a faint, persistent static, not loud enough to name, but sharp enough to feel. It sits in the air like a pressure change before a storm, a reminder of the Disconnect’s lasting truth:

the world still has electricity in places.

It just doesn’t have certainty.
