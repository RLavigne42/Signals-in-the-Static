Neon bleeds into the night like spilled paint, pooling in rainwater trapped in cracked asphalt. The street is a canyon of ruin—storefronts with their windows punched out, awnings hanging in strips, and old posters fused to brick by years of damp and soot. Above it all, a sign fights to stay alive: letters missing, others stuttering, its glow uneven and tired, as if the power feeding it is being rationed by the second.

The buildings around the entrance rise like hollowed teeth. Glass is gone from most frames, leaving black rectangles that swallow light. Vines and windblown grit have claimed the ledges; rust streaks run down concrete in long, dirty tears. A tangle of dead cables droops from a snapped utility pole, swaying slightly, clicking against metal in the breeze—wires that once carried voices now only carry weather.

Across the street, a broken billboard leans at an angle, its face peeled back to reveal warped plywood and a skeleton of corroded supports. The image that remains is sun-bleached and meaningless—an advertisement for a world that assumed tomorrow would always arrive on schedule. Beneath it, the curb is littered with cassette shells, cracked plastic, and the glittering grit of shattered screens ground into the pavement like sand.

Farther down, the city’s skyline is a jagged silhouette: towers with missing corners, rooftop gardens turned feral, and antenna masts bent into question marks. In the distance, a few windows flicker with weak, inconsistent light—brief pulses that suggest a generator catching, then faltering. The air carries the faint tang of ozone and old smoke, the smell of damp concrete, and the metallic bite of rust.

Somewhere behind the walls, bass thumps through brick and rebar, a muffled heartbeat in a district that otherwise feels abandoned. The sound vibrates loose dust from ledges and makes puddles tremble. Above, the rooflines are a layered darkness—vents, water tanks, and collapsed signage forming a serrated edge against a starless sky, as if the city itself is holding its breath.

Everything here looks repurposed, patched, and barely maintained: scavenged panels bolted over gaps, hand-painted warnings fading under grime, and power lines rerouted in crude loops that disappear into shadow. It’s a landscape built from leftovers—half ruin, half refuge—lit by a stubborn glow that refuses to die, even thirty years after the world went quiet.
